# Murch's rule of six, and the blink

From Walter Murch, *In the Blink of an Eye* (Silman-James, 2nd ed. 2001). An ideal cut satisfies all six at once;
when it cannot, give up the criteria from the bottom of the list first — never emotion for story, never story for
rhythm, and so on.

1. **Emotion (51 %)** — is the cut true to the emotion of the moment? What the audience remembers is how it felt.
2. **Story (23 %)** — does it advance the story?
3. **Rhythm (10 %)** — does it occur at a rhythmically right and interesting moment?
4. **Eye-trace (7 %)** — does it respect where the audience's eye is and where it must go in the new frame?
5. **Two-dimensional plane of the screen (5 %)** — screen direction, the 180° line, eyelines.
6. **Three-dimensional space of the action (4 %)** — physical and spatial continuity.

Emotion is weighted above the other five combined. The percentages are Murch's half-serious way of ranking, not a
measurement; they mean: if the emotion is right, the audience forgives a jump in space.

## How the engine maps onto it
The edit report scores every cut on the measurable four (rhythm, eye-trace, plane, space) with Murch's relative
weights ("craft"). Emotion and story are your judgement: read the beat, the line, the performance, the intent the
DP wrote on the shot, and decide whether this is the frame where the feeling is complete.

## Eye-trace in practice
The eye at the end of shot A is where the subject's eyes (or the action) are; the new shot should put its point of
interest near there, or the viewer spends the first frames searching. Large jumps are fine on a long shot and on a
deliberate shock cut; on quick cuts they make the sequence hard to read. Tim Smith's eye-tracking work shows viewers'
gazes synchronise on people, faces, motion and the direction of looks — "attentional synchrony" — which is what lets
an editor predict where the eye will be at the cut.

## The blink
Murch noticed that Gene Hackman, on *The Conversation*, blinked close to the frames where Murch had decided to cut:
a blink marks the end of a thought, the moment attention shifts. The cut is a blink between two thoughts. In his
words in a Q&A, the right cut point is usually a frame or two **before** the actor's blink when the actor is in
character; cutting right after it can be a dramatic choice. Research supports the underlying idea: viewers of the
same video story blink in synchrony at implicit narrative breakpoints, not at random (Nakano et al. 2009). So: cut
when a thought has finished, not in the middle of one.

## Questions to ask at every cut
- What does the audience feel at the last frame of A? Is that feeling complete?
- What do they need next — a reaction, an answer, the object, the place?
- Where is their eye? Where will it land in B?
- Is the thought finished (a breath, a blink, the end of the line, the completion of a gesture)?
- What sound carries across the cut?
