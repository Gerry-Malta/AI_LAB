# Continuity grammar

## Dmytryk's rules of cutting (*On Film Editing*, 1984)
Commonly cited as seven; numbering varies between summaries, the substance does not:
1. Never make a cut without a positive reason. Some scenes need no cutting: composition and camera movement can
   carry them.
2. When undecided about the exact frame, cut long rather than short.
3. Whenever possible, cut in movement (a moving image hides the cut).
4. The "fresh" is preferable to the "stale".
5. All scenes should begin and end with continuing action.
6. Cut for proper values rather than proper "matches" — the dramatic value of a take beats a perfect match.
7. Substance first, then form.

## Continuity editing — the rules and why they work
- **180° rule:** keep the camera on one side of the axis of action so characters keep their screen sides and
  eyelines meet. Crossing needs a bridge (a move across, a neutral axial shot, a character crossing).
- **30° rule:** two shots of the same subject need at least 30° of camera change; otherwise the image "jumps".
  Changing the size two steps on the same axis is the accepted exception (the axial cut, a punch-in). The engine
  flags < 30° with less than a two-step size change.
- **Eyeline match:** a look off-screen followed by what is seen (or by the person looked at) — the look's direction
  and height must agree with where the other is.
- **Match on action:** cut during a movement that continues in the next shot (the hand reaches, the next shot
  completes the reach). The eye follows the motion and does not register the cut.
- **Screen direction:** a character moving left-to-right keeps moving left-to-right in the next shot unless we see
  the change. The engine checks characters moving across a cut.
- **Establishing / re-establishing:** orient before cutting into fragments; re-orient after movement or a turn.
- **Movement into stillness:** a cut from a travelling camera into a static frame is abrupt; let the move settle
  (Layout Studio: `move: [start, end]`) or cut on an action.

Why it works (Tim Smith, the Attentional Theory of Cinematic Continuity): the rules use the cues that already steer
our attention — motion, gaze, pointing, turns of conversation, off-screen sound — to move attention across the cut,
so the cut falls when the eye is busy. Edits that follow the rules are less often noticed ("edit blindness",
Smith & Henderson 2008).

## Jump cuts on purpose
A jump cut is an intentional break: elapsed time, disorientation, a character's mind jumping. If you want it, make it
obvious (same frame, time jumps); a jump cut that looks like a failed continuity cut reads as a mistake.
