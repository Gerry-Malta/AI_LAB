# Lenses, distance and angles

## Focal length and distance
- **Perspective is set by the distance, not the focal length.** A wide lens close to a face swells the nose and
  shrinks the ears; the same frame size from further away on a longer lens flattens the face. A 24 mm close-up
  enlarges the nose and forehead; an 85 mm from conversational distance is the classic portrait compromise.
- **It changes how the face is judged.** Bryan, Perona & Adolphs (2012, PLOS ONE): faces photographed from inside
  personal space were rated less trustworthy, less competent and less attractive, and drew smaller investments in a
  trust game — with image size, expression and lighting controlled, and without viewers noticing the distortion.
  Practical rule: for a sympathetic close-up keep the lens ≥ 1.2 m from the face; go closer and wider only when
  unease is the point.
- **Lens families.** A scene shot on two or three focal lengths has a consistent "look"; reverse singles must share
  one. Intensified continuity (Bordwell) describes contemporary style using longer lenses for singles and OTS and
  wide lenses for emphasis — the bipolar use of lenses.
- **Equivalence.** The engine compares lenses by full-frame equivalent (diagonal). On a Super 35 2.39:1 crop of the
  ALEXA 35 a 50 mm reads like about 71 mm on full frame.

## Camera height and vertical angle
| lens vs eyes | reading | use |
|---|---|---|
| ±5° | eye level | neutral, intimacy, conflict: equals |
| 5–15° above | slightly high | observation, gentle vulnerability |
| 15–35° above | high | vulnerability, isolation, a judged character |
| > 35° above | extreme high / overhead | the map, fate, a figure crushed by the place |
| 5–15° below | slightly low | presence |
| 15–35° below | low | power, threat, heroism |
| > 35° below | extreme low | monumentality, menace |

Evidence: Kraft (1987) found people photographed from below eye height were rated stronger and more active than the
same people photographed from above. Later studies show the picture is less simple — effects are smaller,
context-dependent, and strong angles either way can cost trust; one product-photography result (Meyers-Levy &
Peracchio 1992) did not replicate. Use the angle to support the performance and the story, not as a switch.

A **lowered head** (reading, a phone, a drawing) shows its crown to an eye-level lens. To find the face, the lens
must go lower — the engine compensates by 0.6 of the head pitch and the shot check flags "looking down on the crown"
in close sizes. Sometimes the right answer is to wait for the moment the character looks up.

## Face angle
| off the face axis | reading |
|---|---|
| 0–12° | frontal: direct address, confession, accusation |
| 12–50° | three-quarter: the classical single; both eyes read, the eyeline passes close to the lens |
| 50–75° | three-quarter profile: more detached |
| 75–100° | profile: observation, distance; only one eye reads |
| > 100° | back of the head: the face is not the point (or comes later) |

In dialogue the eyeline should pass close to the lens: the closer the look to the lens, the stronger the moment.

## Dutch angle
A roll of the horizon reads unstable. Use 3–8° for unease, more for a world off its axis; never by accident (the
engine flags any roll outside the intent).

## Depth of field
Shallow focus isolates the subject and tells the eye where to look (intimacy, detail, tension); deep focus lets the
audience read the space and choose (establish, observation, calm). The engine reports the near/far focus limits and
whether the background falls outside them.
