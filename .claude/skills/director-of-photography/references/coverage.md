# Coverage

## The five C's (Mascelli, 1965)
Camera angles, continuity, cutting, close-ups, composition — the DP thinks about all five for every set-up,
because a frame is only good if it cuts. Mascelli's distinction between **objective**, **subjective** (POV) and
**point-of-view** angles (over the shoulder, beside the character) defines the audience's relationship to the
event; choose it per shot, deliberately.

## The axis and the triangle
- **The 180° line** runs between the two characters of a dialogue (or along a direction of movement). All set-ups
  stay on one side, so each character keeps their side of the screen and the eyelines meet. Crossing it is allowed
  with a visible move across the line, a neutral shot on the axis, or a character crossing — never by a plain cut.
  Mark it on the shot: `line: ['CHAR_a', 'CHAR_b']`, and compose with `side: 1 | -1`.
- **The triangle system** (Arijon, *Grammar of the Film Language*): from one side of the line, a master two-shot and
  two "wing" positions form a triangle; its variations give external reverses (over the shoulder), internal reverses
  (clean singles from inside the pair), parallel positions (profiles) and the right-angle set-ups. Build the coverage
  of a dialogue from these, not from random angles.
- **The 30° rule:** two shots of the same subject cut together need at least 30° of camera change (or a two-step
  size change on the same axis — an axial cut). Less reads as a jump cut. The film-editor skill checks it on every
  cut.

## Shot / reverse shot
- Match the pair: **same size, same lens, same eye height, matched look room**, eyelines mirrored. Then the cut is
  invisible and the performances compare fairly. The DP report checks lens, eye height, angle, face angle and the
  look of every reverse pair.
- **Dirty (OTS) vs clean singles:** an over-the-shoulder keeps both people in the frame — connection, geography —
  and is the usual start of a dialogue. Clean singles isolate and intensify; save them for the tighter, more
  emotional beats. A common progression: master → OTS pair → clean MCUs → clean CUs at the peak.
- Look into the lens zone: the closer the eyeline passes to the lens, the more intense the moment.

## The same character twice in a row
Two consecutive shots of the same person at the same size, both matched to the reverse, sit almost on the same set-up:
the cut jumps. Choose: one set-up (a split take — the editor joins it as one shot), a two-step size change on the same
axis (an axial cut, MCU → BCU), a 30°+ move that you then match on the other side too, or a reaction / insert between
them.

## Establishing and re-establishing
Establish geography before cutting into singles (the audience needs to know who is where to read eyelines). When
someone moves, enters, or the scene turns, re-establish with a wider frame. Intensified continuity uses fewer
establishing shots because wide formats let close singles keep the room visible — but the audience must still be
oriented.

## Sizes over a scene
Sizes are a scale of intimacy and intensity. Plan them as a curve: start wide (orientation, affinity), tighten
toward the turning point (contrast), open again at the release. Hitchcock's rule decides the inserts: an object gets
its close-up when the story needs that object now.

## Point of view
A subjective shot (the lens is the character's eyes, at their eye height and on their eyeline) followed by the
character's reaction is the strongest identification device (and the Kuleshov effect means the reaction will be
read through what was seen). Use `childhood` for a child's POV height.
